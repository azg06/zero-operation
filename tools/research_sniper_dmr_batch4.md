The '''M14 rifle''' is an American [[battle rifle]] chambered for the [[7.62×51mm NATO]] [[rifle cartridge|cartridge]]. It was accepted as the [[service rifle]] of the [[United States Armed Forces]] in 1957, with production starting in 1959. The M14 replaced the [[Semi-automatic rifle|semi-automatic]] [[M1 Garand]] in US service.<ref>Thompson, Leroy, ''The M14 Battle Rifle'' Osprey Publishing (2014), pp.8-9</ref>

In 1967, it was officially replaced as the US service rifle by the [[M16 rifle|M16]] [[assault rifle]], a lighter weapon with a smaller [[5.56×45mm NATO|5.56×45mm]] [[intermediate cartridge]]. The M14 remained in use by the [[United States Army|U.S. Army]], [[United States Navy|Navy]], and [[United States Marine Corps|Marine Corps]] for Basic and Advanced Individual Training from the mid-1960s to the early 1970s.<ref name="FM23-8_U.S._Rifle"/><ref name="Operator_Manual">{{cite book |url=https://www.barnesandnoble.com/w/tm-9-1005-223-10-operators-manual-for-rifle-762mm-m14-w-e-and-m14a1-w-e-department-of-defense/1134244996 |title=TM 9-1005-223-10 Operator's Manual for Rifle, 7.62mm, M14, W/E and M14A1, W/E |location=Washington, DC |publisher=Department of Defense |date=1972 |isbn=978-1-98128-407-8}}</ref>

The M14 rifle remains in limited service across all branches of the U.S. military, with variants used as [[sniper rifle|sniper]] and [[designated marksman rifle]]s, accurized competition weapons, and [[ceremonial weapon]]s by [[honor guard]]s, [[color guard]]s, [[drill team]]s, and ceremonial guards. Civilian semi-automatic variants are used for hunting, target shooting, and shooting competitions.<ref name="FM23-8_U.S._Rifle" />

The M14 served as the basis for the [[M21 Sniper Weapon System|M21]]<ref name="M21">{{cite web |url=http://www.snipercentral.com/us-army-m21-xm21/ |title=US Army M21 and XM21 |last=Ewing |first=Mel |date=2016-04-28 |website=SniperCentral.com |access-date=26 February 2017 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20170115143644/http://www.snipercentral.com/us-army-m21-xm21/ |archive-date=15 January 2017 |url-status=dead}}</ref> and [[M25 sniper rifle|M25]] sniper rifles, which were eventually replaced by the [[M24 Sniper Weapon System]]. A new variant of the M14, the [[Mk 14 Enhanced Battle Rifle]], has been in very limited service since 2002.<ref name="AmericanRifleman-M14EBR">{{cite web |url=https://www.americanrifleman.org/articles/2011/2/23/the-m14-enhanced-battle-rifle/ |title=The M14 Enhanced Battle Rifle |last=Plaster |first=John L. |date=February 23, 2011 |website=American Rifleman |access-date=26 February 2017}}</ref>

==History==

===Early development===
The M14's development stemmed from an extensive line of experimental weapons based on the M1 Garand rifle. Although the M1 was among the most advanced [[infantry]] rifle designs of the late 1930s, it was not without faults, as various modifications to the basic M1 were considered during the last months of [[World War II]]. Changes included the addition of [[Automatic firearm|fully automatic]] fire and the replacement of the eight-round [[Clip (ammunition)#En bloc|''en bloc'' clip]] with a detachable [[box magazine]] holding 20 rounds. [[Winchester Repeating Arms Company|Winchester]], [[Remington Arms|Remington]], and [[Springfield Armory]]'s [[John Garand]] offered alternate conversions. Garand's design, the [[M1 Garand rifle#Variants|T20]], was the most popular, and T20 prototypes served as the basis for several [[Springfield rifle|Springfield]] test rifles from 1945 to the early 1950s.<ref>{{cite book |last=Lewis |first=Jack |author-link=Jack Lewis (screenwriter) |title=Gun Digest Book of Assault Weapons |edition=7th |year=2007 |publisher=Gun Digest Books |location=Iola, Wisconsin |isbn=978-1-4402-2652-6 |page=210 |chapter=A short-lived replacement}}</ref>

[[File:T25 Experimental Rifle.jpg|thumb|left|upright=1.25|T25 prototype]]
In 1945, Earle Harvey of [[Springfield Armory]] designed an entirely different rifle, the T25, for the new T65 .30 light rifle [[Cartridge (firearms)|cartridge]] (7.62×49mm). This was at the direction of Col. Rene Studler, then serving in the Pentagon.<ref name="RAY">{{cite book |url=https://www.merriam-press.com/randomshots.aspx |last=Rayle |first=Roy E. |title=Random Shots: Episodes In The Life Of A Weapons Developer |location=Bennington, VT |publisher=Merriam Press |isbn=978-1-4357-5021-0 |date=2008 |edition=4th |pages=17–22, 95–95 |access-date=2019-12-15 |archive-date=2019-12-15 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20191215223605/https://www.merriam-press.com/randomshots.aspx |url-status=dead}}</ref> Harvey and Studler were then transferred to Springfield Armory in late 1945, to continuously develop the T25.<ref name="RAY"/> The T25 was designed to use the T65 service cartridge, a Frankford Arsenal design based upon the [[.30-06 Springfield|.30-06]] cartridge used in the M1, but shortened to the same length as the [[.300 Savage]].<ref name="RAY" />

Although shorter than the .30-06, meaning less powder capacity, the T65 cartridge retained the ballistics and energy of the .30-06, which can be attributed to the use of a recently developed ball powder designed by Olin Industries.<ref name="RAY" /><ref name="Modern Firearms M14" /> After experimenting with several bullet designs, the T65 was finalized for adoption as the [[7.62×51mm NATO]] cartridge.<ref name="RAY" /> Olin Industries later introduced the cartridge to the commercial market as the [[.308 Winchester]].<ref name="RAY"/> After a series of revisions by Earle Harvey and the other members of the .30 light rifle design group following the 1950 [[Fort Benning]] tests, the T25 was renamed the T47.<ref name="RAY"/>

The T44 prototype rifle was a conventional design developed at Springfield Armory as an alternative to the T47.<ref name="RAY"/> With minimal funding available, the earliest T44 prototypes used T20E2 [[Receiver (firearms)|receivers]] fitted with magazine filler blocks and rebarreled for the 7.62×51mm cartridge. The long operating rod/piston of the M1 was replaced by the T47's gas cut-off system.<ref name="RAY"/> Though not principally designed by any single engineer, Lloyd Corbett is credited for several refinements to the T44 design. Refinements included the addition of a bolt roller intended to reduce friction and a straight operating rod.<ref name="RAY"/>

===Infantry Board service rifle trials===
[[File:T47 Experimental Rifle.jpg|thumb|left|upright=1.25|An experimental T47 rifle]]

The T44 was tested in a competitive service rifle competition conducted by the Infantry Board at Fort Benning, Georgia against the Springfield T47 (a modified T25) and the [[T48 rifle|T48]], a variant of [[Fabrique Nationale]]'s [[FN FAL]].<ref name="STE">{{cite book |last=Stevens |first=R. Blake |title=The FAL Rifle |publisher=Collector Grade Publications |isbn=978-0-88935-168-4 |date=1993 |edition=Classic}}</ref> The T47 did not have a bolt roller and performed worse than both the T44 and the T48 in dust and cold-weather tests. Thus, it was dropped from consideration in 1953.<ref name="RAY"/> During 1952–53, testing proved the T48 and the T44 to be roughly comparable in performance, with the T48 holding an advantage in ease of field stripping and dust resistance, and a longer product development lead time.<ref name="RAY"/><ref name="STE"/>

During the winter of 1953–54, both rifles competed in the winter rifle trials at U.S. Army facilities in the Arctic.<ref name="STE"/><ref name="CRU">{{cite web |url=http://www.cruffler.com/historic-april01.html |title=The T48 Automatic Rifle: The American FAL |date=April 2001 |website=Cruffler.com |access-date=24 April 2012}}</ref> Springfield Armory engineers, anxious to ensure the selection of the T44, had been specially preparing and modifying the test T44 rifles for weeks with the aid of the armory's cold chamber, including a redesign of the T44 gas regulator and custom modifications to magazines and other parts to reduce friction and seizing in extreme cold.<ref name="STE"/><ref name="CRU"/>

The T48 rifles received no such special preparation and in the continued cold-weather testing began to experience sluggish gas system functioning, aggravated by the T48's close-fitting surfaces between bolt and carrier, and carrier and receiver.<ref name="RAY"/><ref name="STE"/><ref name="CRU"/> FN engineers opened the gas ports in an attempt to improve functioning, but this caused early/violent extraction and broken parts as a result of the increased pressures.<ref name="RAY"/><ref name="STE"/><ref name="CRU"/> As a result, the T44 was ranked superior in cold-weather operation to the T48.<ref name="STE"/> The Arctic Test Board report made it clear that the T48 needed improvement and that the U.S. would not adopt the T48 until it had successfully completed another round of Arctic tests the following winter.<ref name="RAY"/><ref name="STE"/>

In June 1954, funding became available to manufacture newly fabricated T44 receivers specially designed for the shorter T65 cartridge.<ref name="RAY"/> This single change to the T44 design saved a pound over the M1 Garand.<ref name="RAY"/> Tests at Fort Benning with the T44 and T48 continued through the summer and fall of 1956.<ref name="RAY"/> By this time, the T48/FAL rifles had been so improved that malfunction rates were almost as low as the T44.<ref name="RAY"/>

The T44 was selected over the T48/FAL due to lower weight, simplicity with fewer parts, the T44's self-compensating gas system, and because the T44 could supposedly be manufactured on existing machinery built for the M1 rifle (which was later found to be false).<ref name="RAY"/><ref name="STE"/><ref name="CRU"/><ref name="HAT">{{cite book |last=Hatcher |first=Julian S. |author-link=Julian Hatcher |date=1962 |title=Hatcher's Notebook |edition=3rd |location=Harrisburg, PA |publisher=The Stackpole Company |page=496}}</ref> In 1957, the U.S. formally adopted the T44 as the U.S. infantry service rifle designated M14.<ref name="RAY"/>

===Production contracts===
Initial production contracts for the M14 were awarded to the [[Springfield Armory]], [[Winchester Repeating Arms Company|Winchester]], and [[H & R Firearms|Harrington & Richardson]].<ref name="Lee_Emerson"/> [[TRW Inc.|Thompson-Ramo-Wooldridge Inc.]] (TRW) was later awarded a production contract for the rifle, as well. In all, 1,376,031 M14 service rifles were produced from 1959 to 1964.<ref name="EME">Emerson (2007), pp. 11, 29, 37, 60-72.</ref>

====National Match M14====
Springfield Armory produced 6,641 new M14 NM rifles in 1962 and 1963. TRW produced 4,874 new M14 NM rifles in 1964. Springfield Armory upgraded 2,094 M14 rifles in 1965 and 2,395 M14 rifles in 1966 to National Match specifications. 2,462 M14 rifles were rebuilt to National Match standards in 1967 at the [[Rock Island Arsenal]]. In total, 11,130 National Match rifles were delivered by Springfield Armory, Rock Island Arsenal, and TRW during 1962–1967.<ref name="EME"/>

Production M14 rifles made by Springfield Armory and Winchester used forged receivers and bolts milled from AISI 8620 steel, a low-carbon steel with added molybdenum-chromium. Harrington & Richardson M14 production used AISI 8620 steel, as well, except for 10 receivers milled from AISI 1330 low-carbon steel and a single receiver made from a high-nickel alloy steel.<ref name="EME" />

===Deployment===
[[File:Guantanamo Bay Haircut 1962.jpg|thumb|left|Two soldiers with M14s at [[Guantanamo Bay Naval Base]] in 1962]]
After the M14's adoption, Springfield Armory began tooling a new production line in 1958, delivering the first service rifles to the U.S. Army in July 1959. Long production delays resulted in the [[101st Airborne Division]] being the only unit in the army fully equipped with the M14 by the end of 1960. The [[Fleet Marine Force]] completed the change from M1s to M14s in late 1961. Springfield Armory records reflect that M14 manufacture ended as TRW, fulfilling its second contract, delivered its final production increment in the fiscal year 1965 (1 July 1964 – 30 June 1965). The Springfield archive indicates the 1.38 million rifles were acquired for just over $143 million, for a unit cost of about $104.<ref name=book1_pp20-21/><ref name=book2_pp245/>

The M14 was developed to replace four different weapons: the M1 Garand, the [[M3 submachine gun]], the [[M1 carbine]], and the [[M1918 Browning automatic rifle]] (BAR).<ref>{{cite book |last=Thompson |first=Leroy |title=The M14 Battle Rifle |series=Weapons |volume=37 |date=2014 |page=12 |publisher=[[Osprey Publishing]] |isbn=978-1-47280-255-2}}</ref> The intention was to simplify the logistical requirements of the troops by limiting the types of ammunition and parts needed to be supplied. Replacing all these weapons with a single weapon system proved to be an impossible task. The M14 was deemed "completely inferior" to even the World War II M1 Garand in a September 1962 report by the [[comptroller]] for the [[United States Department of Defense|U.S. Department of Defense]].<ref name="dtic.mil">{{cite web |url=https://apps.dtic.mil/sti/pdfs/ADA044796.pdf |title=An Analysis of the Infantry's Need for an Assault Submachine Gun |last=Kay |first=Bruce I. |date=10 June 1977 |page=9 |url-status=live |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20090619194146/http://www.dtic.mil/cgi-bin/GetTRDoc?AD=ADA044796&Location=U2&doc=GetTRDoc.pdf |archive-date=June 19, 2009}}</ref>

The '''M24 Sniper Weapon System''' ('''SWS''') or '''M24''' is the military and police version of the [[Remington Model 700]] rifle, ''M24'' being the model name assigned by the [[United States Army]] after adoption as their standard [[sniper rifle]] in 1988. The M24 is referred to as a "weapon system" because it consists of not only a rifle, but also a detachable [[telescopic sight]] and other accessories.

The M24 SWS has the "long action" bolt version of the Remington 700 receiver but is chambered for the [[7.62×51mm NATO]] "short action" cartridge that has an overall length of {{convert|2.750|in|mm|2}}. The "long action" allows the rifle to be re-configured for dimensionally larger cartridges up to {{convert|3.340|in|mm|2}} in overall length.

The M24 originally came tapped for the Leupold Ultra M3A 10×42mm fixed-power scope, which came with a circle-shaped mil-dot glass-etched reticle. This was later replaced in 1998 by the Leupold Mk 4 LR/T M1 10×40mm fixed-power scope with an elongated-shaped mil-dot wire reticle.<ref>{{Cite web |url=http://www.leupold.com/tactical/products/scopes/mark-4-lrt-riflescopes/mark-4-10x40mm-lrt-m1 |title=Focal plane Leupold Mk 4 LR/T M1 10*40mm |website=Leupold & Stevens, Inc. |access-date=May 11, 2015 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20120615233754/http://www.leupold.com/tactical/products/scopes/mark-4-lrt-riflescopes/mark-4-10x40mm-lrt-m1 |archive-date=June 15, 2012 |url-status=dead}}</ref> The rifle also comes with a detachable Harris 9–13" 1A2-LM or Harris 9–13" 1A2-L bipod unit.

The M24 SWS was to be replaced with the [[M110 Semi-Automatic Sniper System]], a contract awarded to [[Knight's Armament Company]]. However, the Army still continued to acquire M24s from Remington until February 2010 and upgraded to the A2 and M24E1 standard in many cases, continuing to serve.<ref>{{cite web |url=http://www.defenselink.mil/contracts/contract.aspx?contractid=3820 |title=Contracts |date=July 16, 2008 |website=DefenseLink |publisher=U.S. Department of Defense |url-status=dead |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20090513050635/http://www.defenselink.mil/contracts/contract.aspx?contractid=3820 |archive-date=May 13, 2009}}</ref> The Army chose to upgrade all its M24 rifles in the arsenals to the [[M2010 Enhanced Sniper Rifle]], with the final M24 being converted in April 2014.<ref name="peosoldier.armylive.dodlive.mil">{{cite web |url=http://peosoldier.armylive.dodlive.mil/2014/05/09/snipers-put-finishing-touches-on-the-last-sniper-rifle/ |title=Snipers Put Finishing Touches on the Last Sniper Rifle |first=D. |last=Dawson |date=May 9, 2014 |website=[[PEO Soldier]] Live |url-status=dead |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20141011211520/http://peosoldier.armylive.dodlive.mil/2014/05/09/snipers-put-finishing-touches-on-the-last-sniper-rifle/ |archive-date=October 11, 2014}}</ref>

==Specifications==
{{more citations needed section|date=January 2013}}
[[File:061019-A-7603F-151.jpg|left|thumb|U.S. Army sniper team with the M24 SWS]]

[[Telescopic sight|Scope]]: [[Leupold & Stevens|Leupold]] Ultra M3A 10×42mm fixed power, or Leupold Mk 4 LR/T M3 10×40mm fixed power [[Telescopic sight|scope]]. Detachable emergency Redfield-Palma International back-up [[iron sights]], they are attached to iron sight bases that are screwed into drilled and tapped holes machined into the front of the barrel and back on the left side of the receiver. In 2001 when Remington had the second contract to the U.S. military, minor changes were done to the M24. These changes included two piece Leupold Mark 4 scope ring bases instead of the one piece on the first series rifles, and a switch from Redfield-Palma International iron sights to another manufacturer OK Weber. Redfield was out of business by then, necessitating a change.

[[Gun barrel|Barrel]]: Made of [[SAE steel grades#Stainless steel|416R stainless steel]]. The bore twist is 1-turn-in-11.25&nbsp;inches [1:285.75&nbsp;mm] and the rifling is five radial lands and grooves (5-R) with a right-hand (RH) twist. Because of the odd number of lands, none of the lands are 180° apart, i.e. in direct opposition. This results in less bullet deformation, which (at least in theory) produces more consistent point of impact. In 5-R rifling, the "side" of the land is cut at a 65° angle, rather than 90° in conventional rifling. This results in less barrel fouling, and more consistent point of impact, compared to conventional rifling when relatively high numbers of rounds are fired between cleaning, as might be expected in military applications.

[[Stock (firearm)|Stock]]: H-S Precision PST-024, the model with the fixed cheek piece, adjustable LOP and hinged floorplate. The stock secures the action via an aluminum bedding block to keep it rigid. The barrel is free floating. A twin-strut extension that pulls out of the butt allows a {{convert|2|in|mm|adj=on}} adjustment to length-of-pull, as well as allowing for the weapon to fit inside the M-1950 Weapon Container for Airborne Operations. This is effected by a thick wheel on a central grooved pillar for adjusting the length and a thin locking ring behind it locks the thick wheel in place. The stock is primarily made from a polymer foam reinforced with fiberglass, carbon fiber, and Kevlar to reduce weight and then painted with a heavy duty polane paint. They were rigorously tested before being approved by Remington and the military.

==M24 variants==
===XM24A1===
An experimental variant re-chambered for the [[.300 Winchester Magnum]] (7.62×67mm) round. It was not adopted by the US Army due to concerns that operators would not be able to acquire the special ammo. Also, the available .300 Winchester Magnum ammo that was procured sometimes misfired due to incompletely-burned propellant in the longer cartridge. Redick Arms Development further researched this concept, which eventually grew into the RAD M91A1 (7.62x51mm NATO) and M91A2 (.300 Winchester Magnum). These rifles were adopted by the [[United States Navy SEALs|US Navy SEALs]].

===M24A2===
[[Remington Arms|Remington]] has developed an improved version of the M24 rifle, known as the M24A2. The A2 model features a detachable 5-round [[Magazine (firearm)|magazine]], top-mounted and adjustable side-mounted [[Picatinny rail]]s (advertised by Remington as "MARS", or Modular Accessory Rail System), barrel modifications designed to accommodate a [[sound suppressor]], and an improved H-S Precision PST-026 stock with adjustable cheek height and [[length of pull]]. Existing M24s can also be converted into M24A2s, which replaces the stock and adds the new detachable magazine feed. The conversion requires a re-barreling of the weapon so it can take the OPS silencer.

Remington developed the M24A3 SWS, a variant of the M24 chambered for the [[.338 Lapua Magnum]] round (8.58×70mm) and feeding from a 5-round detachable box magazine.<ref>{{cite web |url=http://www.remingtonmilitary.com/m24a3sws.htm |title=Remington M24A3 SWS |website=Remington Military |url-status=dead |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20061017222046/http://www.remingtonmilitary.com/m24a3sws.htm |archive-date=October 17, 2006}}</ref> It comes tapped for the Leupold Mk 4 M1LR/T 8.5–25×50mm Variable Power [[Telescopic sight|day scope]]. The '''M2010 Enhanced Sniper Rifle''' ('''ESR'''), formerly known as the '''XM2010''' and '''M24 Reconfigured Sniper Weapon System''', is a [[bolt action]] [[sniper rifle]] developed by [[PEO Soldier]] for the [[United States Army]].<ref>{{cite web|title=XM2010|url=https://peosoldier.army.mil/newpeo/Equipment/Temp.asp?id=IW_XM2010|access-date=10 December 2010}}{{dead link|date=April 2025|bot=medic}}{{cbignore|bot=medic}}</ref> It is derived from and replaced the [[M24 Sniper Weapon System]], and was designed to give snipers longer range in the mountainous and desert terrain of the [[War in Afghanistan (2001–present)|War in]] [[Afghanistan]].<ref name="usatoday.com">{{cite web|url=https://www.usatoday.com/news/military/2010-12-09-sniper09_ST_N.htm|title=New rifles give Army snipers in Afghanistan needed range - USATODAY.com|website=[[USA Today]]|access-date=25 December 2014}}</ref> After winning a bidding process, Remington was awarded the production contract for up to 3,600 weapons. The Army had anticipated sending the upgraded weapons to deployed snipers in late 2010,<ref name="December">{{cite web|title=Army awards contract for upgraded sniper weapon system|url=https://www.army.mil/-news/2010/10/01/45970-army-awards-contract-for-upgraded-sniper-weapon-system/|access-date=3 October 2010|author=PEO Soldier|date=1 October 2010}}</ref><ref name="pdf">{{cite web|url=http://www.dtic.mil/ndia/2010armament/TuesdayLandmarkBTamilio.pdf|title=Project Manager Soldier Weapons Briefing for NDIA|last=Fuller|first=Peter N.|author2=Douglas A. Tamilio|date=18 May 2010|work=[[PEO Soldier]]|publisher=United States Army|access-date=28 October 2010|url-status=dead|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20111114024552/http://www.dtic.mil/ndia/2010armament/TuesdayLandmarkBTamilio.pdf|archive-date=14 November 2011|df=dmy-all}}</ref> but later expected fielding would happen in January 2011.<ref name="armymil15oct10"/> The M2010 fires [[.300 Winchester Magnum]] (7.62×67mm) ammunition that offers about 50 percent greater effective range than the M24's [[7.62×51mm NATO]]. This chambering to dimensionally larger cartridges is possible because the M24 was designed to use the "long action" bolt version of the [[Remington 700]] receiver for cartridges up to {{convert|3.34|in|mm|2}} in overall length.

==History==
The [[Barrett M107]] [[.50 BMG]] rifle can hit targets past {{convert|2000|m|yd|0|abbr=on}}, but it is accurate to 2.5 [[Minute of angle|MOA]], meaning it would hit within a {{convert|25|in|mm|abbr=on}} area at {{convert|1000|m|yd|0|abbr=on}}. This was acceptable for shooting at materiel, but not people. The XM2010 addressed the problem with a .300 Winchester Magnum round that can hit targets out to {{convert|1200|m|yd|0|abbr=on}} with a 1 MOA accuracy, half again further than the M24's {{convert|800|m|yd|0|abbr=on}}.

On 20 September 2010, the Army gave Remington a $28 million contract to rebuild 3,600 M24 rifles. By January, 250 had been ordered to be changed.

The U.S. Army issued three XM2010s to snipers at the [[United States Army Sniper School]] on 18 January 2011<ref>{{cite news|url=http://www.marinecorpstimes.com/news/2011/03/marine-corps-sniper-rifle-m110-031711w/|title=Corps fielding new semi-automatic sniper rifle|author=Dan Lamothe|date=17 January 2011|access-date=23 March 2011|url-status=dead|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20110714034725/http://www.marinecorpstimes.com/news/2011/03/marine-corps-sniper-rifle-m110-031711w/|archive-date=14 July 2011|df=dmy-all}}</ref> and began using the rifle in combat in Afghanistan in March 2011.<ref name="usatoday.com" /><ref name="peosoldier.army.mil" /> Snipers in the field learned how to use and maintain the new rifle during a three-day course. After the course, snipers had no difficulty hitting targets out to 1,000 meters from "ridgetop to ridgetop". In addition to the more powerful cartridge, the new optic improves the rifle's ability to sight a target quickly without calculations of range estimation.

All 250 XM2010 rifles were to be fielded in eight Brigade Combat Teams by mid-May 2011.<ref>{{cite web |title=XM2010 'Nailing Fellows' in Afghanistan |url=http://kitup.military.com/2011/04/xm2010-nailing-fellows-in-afghanistan.html |publisher=Kitup.Military.com |date=28 April 2011}}</ref> Based on the results and feedback from troops, the U.S. Army decided in May 2011 to replace its entire fleet of M24s, ordering a total of 2,558 M2010 rifles.<ref>{{cite web |title=Army Leaning Toward XM2010 for All |url=http://kitup.military.com/2011/04/army-leaning-toward-xm2010-for-all.html |publisher=Kitup.Military.com |date=15 April 2011}}</ref><ref name="armytimes25april2011" /> By September 2012, the Army had fielded more than 1,400 systems as part of an urgent material release. The M2010 achieved Type Classification-Standard in July 2013 and Full Materiel Release in September 2013, supporting procurement for the balance of the Army requirement. On 25 April 2014, the 2,558th M2010 rifle was completed.<ref name="peosoldier.army.mil">{{cite web |url=http://peosoldier.armylive.dodlive.mil/2014/05/09/snipers-put-finishing-touches-on-the-last-sniper-rifle/ |title=Snipers Put Finishing Touches on the Last Sniper Rifle |publisher=peosoldier.armylive.dodlive.mil |date=9 May 2014 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20141011211520/http://peosoldier.armylive.dodlive.mil/2014/05/09/snipers-put-finishing-touches-on-the-last-sniper-rifle/ |archive-date=11 October 2014}}</ref><ref>{{cite web |title=Remington Completes Its M2010 Contract |url=http://www.tactical-life.com/firearms/remington-m2010/?scrape=true |publisher=tactical-life.com |date=14 May 2014 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20160624195556/http://www.tactical-life.com/firearms/remington-m2010/?scrape=true |archive-date=24 June 2016}}</ref>

===Precision guided firearm testing===
In January 2014, the Army bought six "smart scopes" made by [[TrackingPoint]] for testing on the M2010 sniper rifle. Costing between $22,000 and $27,000, the computerized scope marks a selected target, gathers and compensates for external factors, and uses a special trigger that does not pull until the system is sure the bullet will land where intended. It can help a bolt-action rifle to hit targets out to {{convert|1,250|yd|m|0|abbr=on}}. It is intended to help non-snipers take shots at longer ranges, not replace sniping skills.<ref>{{cite web |title=Army Testing Smart Scope on .300 Win Mag Sniper Rifle |url=http://kitup.military.com/2014/03/army-testing-smart-scope-300-win.html |publisher=Kitup.Military.com |date=14 March 2014}}</ref>

The '''SVD''' (СВД; {{langx|ru|снайперская винтовка Драгунова|lit=Dragunov sniper rifle|translit=snayperskaya vintovka Dragunova}}), [[GRAU]] index '''6V1''',<ref>{{Cite web |date=2023-07-05 |title=Первая в мире. Снайперская винтовка Драгунова {{!}} Оружейный журнал "КАЛАШНИКОВ" |url=https://www.kalashnikov.ru/pervaya-v-mire-snajperskaya-vintovka-dragunova/ |access-date=2023-07-28 |language=ru-RU}}</ref> is a [[Semi-automatic rifle|semi-automatic]] [[designated marksman rifle]]/[[sniper rifle]]<ref>{{Cite web |title=Снайперская винтовка Драгунова СВД 7,62 мм |url=http://izhmash.ru/rus/product/svd.shtml |website=Izhmash.ru|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20141018125650/http://izhmash.ru/rus/product/svd.shtml |archive-date=2014-10-18 }}</ref> [[Chamber (firearms)|chambered]] in the 7.62×54mmR cartridge, developed in the [[Soviet Union]].

==History==
The SVD was designed to serve in a [[squad]] support role to provide precise long-range engagement capabilities to ordinary troops following the [[Warsaw Pact]] adoption of the [[7.62×39mm]] [[intermediate cartridge]] and [[assault rifle]]s as standard infantry weapon systems. At the time, [[NATO]] used [[Battle rifle#Cold War|battle rifle]]s chambered for the [[7.62×51mm NATO]] [[fully powered cartridge]] as standard infantry weapon systems and had not yet adopted an intermediate cartridge and assault rifle of their own, allowing them to outrange their Warsaw Pact counterparts.<ref name="krtraining1">{{cite magazine |author1=Kjellgren, G. L. M. |url=http://www.krtraining.com/KRTraining/Archive/PracticalRangeSmallArms.pdf|title=The Practical Range of Small Arms|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20150305142959/http://www.krtraining.com/KRTraining/Archive/PracticalRangeSmallArms.pdf |archive-date=5 March 2015 |magazine=The American Rifleman |pages= 40–44|url-status=live}}</ref>

The SVD was developed through 1958–1963 and selected as the winner of a contest that included three competing groups of designers, led by [[Sergei Gavrilovich Simonov|Sergei Simonov]] (prototype rejected in April 1960), Aleksandr Konstantinov, and [[Yevgeny Dragunov]]. Extensive field testing of the rifles conducted in a wide range of environmental conditions (Konstantinov's competing 2B-W-10 prototype was simpler and cheaper but less accurate, durable and reliable) resulted in Dragunov's proposal chambered for the 7.62×54mmR fully powered cartridge being accepted into service in July 1963.<ref name="milmag.pl">{{Cite web|url=https://milmag.pl/en/the-dragunov-replacement-for-poland-new-762x51-mm-marksman-rifle/|title=The Dragunov Replacement for Poland: New 7,62x51 mm marksman rifle|first=Remigiusz|last=Wilk|date=October 21, 2018|website=MILMAG}}</ref> An initial pre-production batch consisting of 200 rifles was assembled for evaluation purposes, and from 1964 serial production was carried out by Izhmash, later called [[Kalashnikov Concern]].

[[File:Chukavin SVCH sniper rifle in Russian pavilion at Dubai Airshow 2023.jpg|thumb|left|The Chukavin SVCh is intended to replace the SVD in Russian service]]

Since then, the SVD has become the standard squad support weapon of several countries, including those of the former Warsaw Pact. China produced a copy of the SVD through [[reverse-engineered]] samples captured during the [[Sino-Vietnamese War]] as the Type 79 and 85.<ref>{{Cite web |url=https://warisboring.com/chinese-sniper-rifles-have-a-troubled-history/ |title=Chinese Sniper Rifles Have a Troubled History |date=4 June 2018 |access-date=2019-04-03 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20190403023538/https://warisboring.com/chinese-sniper-rifles-have-a-troubled-history/ |archive-date=2019-04-03 |url-status=live }}</ref> Iran also produced a clone, the Nakhjir 3, which was a direct copy of the Chinese Type 79.

In Russian service, the SVD is to be replaced with the [[Chukavin sniper rifle|Chukavin SVCh]] sniper/designated marksman rifle. In February 2023, it was reported that the Chukavin SVCh began to be mass-produced by [[Kalashnikov Concern]].<ref>{{Cite web |title=В "Калашникове" сообщили о начале серийного производства снайперской винтовки Чукавина |url=https://tass.ru/armiya-i-opk/17099989 |access-date=2023-12-22 |website=TACC}}</ref> When chambered for the 7.62×54mmR, the Chukavin SVCh uses SVD compatible box magazines.<ref>{{Cite web |title="Калашников" завершил квалификационные испытания СВЧ |url=https://rostec.ru/news/kalashnikov-zavershil-kvalifikatsionnye-ispytaniya-svch-/?sphrase_id=4982269 |access-date=2023-11-02 |website=rostec.ru}}</ref><ref>{{Cite web|url=https://modernfirearms.net/en/sniper-rifles/svch-sniper-designated-marksman-2/|title=SVCh Sniper Rifle|date=November 3, 2017|website=Modern Firearms}}</ref><ref>{{Cite web|url=https://en.kalashnikovgroup.ru/media/boevoe-strelkovoe-oruzhie/snayperskaya-vintovka-chukavina|title=Kalashnikov group SVCh rifles line|accessdate=26 June 2025}}</ref>

In Ukrainian service, the SVD was largely replaced in the sniping role with Western sniper rifles chambered in [[.338 Lapua Magnum]] or 7.62×51mm NATO cartridges and [[anti-materiel rifle]]s such as the [[Barrett M107A1]].{{sfn|McNab|2023|page=71}}<ref>{{cite news |last1=Korshak |first1=Stefan |title=How Ukraine's Most Elite Snipers Rewrote the Marksman's Handbook |url=https://www.kyivpost.com/post/20152 |access-date=14 July 2024 |work=Kyiv Post |date=3 August 2023 |language=en}}</ref> The domestically produced [[UAR-10]] sniper rifle is also replacing the SVD in Ukrainian military and law enforcement units.{{sfn|McNab|2023|pages=71−72}} According to McNab, the SVD remains in service with Ukrainian forces in the designated marksman rifle role.{{sfn|McNab|2023|page=72}}

==Design==
The SVD bears a number of cosmetic similarities to the [[Kalashnikov rifle|AK]] family of rifles but these similarities are for the purpose of standardizing manual of arms. This has occasionally led to misidentification of the SVD as an AK variant, and vice versa.

=== Operating mechanism ===
The barrel breech is locked through a [[rotating bolt]] (left rotation) and uses three locking lugs to engage corresponding locking recesses in the barrel extension. The rifle has a hammer-type striking mechanism and a manual lever safety selector. In addition to the trigger disconnect, the fire control mechanism has a second disconnector which does not allow the hammer to fall until the bolt has been closed, similar to a [[Sear (firearm)|sear]] in a select-fire weapon. However, the SVD was only designed for semi-automatic fire. The firing pin in the SVD is not retained, i.e. "free-floating", and it is therefore possible for accidental discharge to occur as the bolt pushes an unfired cartridge into the chamber, should there be an obstruction in the firing pin channel resulting from poor maintenance or extreme cold.

The firearm is operated by a [[Gas-operated reloading|short-stroke gas piston]] system with a two-position gas regulator. The gas regulator can be set with the help of the rim of a cartridge. Position #1 leaves a gas escape port opened, whereas position #2 closes the gas escape port and directs extra gas to the piston, increasing the recoil velocity of the gas-piston system and is used for resolving reliability issues, which arise from fouling in the gas port/action, extreme cold, high altitude, or using under-powered ammunition.

The rifle is fed from a detachable curved [[Magazine (firearms)#Box|box magazine]] with a 10-round capacity and the cartridges are double-stacked in a staggered zigzag pattern. After discharging the last cartridge from the magazine, the bolt carrier and bolt are held back on a bolt catch that is released by pulling the cocking handle to the rear.

The rifle's [[Receiver (firearms)|receiver]] is [[machining|machined]] to improve precision by adding [[torsion (mechanics)|torsional]] strength.

=== Barrel ===
The [[Gun barrel|barrel]] profile is relatively thin to save weight. Its bore is [[chromium|chrome]]-lined for increased corrosion resistance and features four right-hand grooves. Originally, the [[twist rate]] was 320&nbsp;mm (1:12.6 in), as it had been designed for use with heavier civilian ammunition. In 1975 the twist rate was increased to the standard 240&nbsp;mm (1:9.4 in), which reduced the precision with the 7N1 sniper cartridge by 19% but allowed for the use of standard "light" ball steel core LPS Gzh (57-N-323S), as well as its variations (incendiary, tracer, armour-piercing) with acceptable precision.<ref>{{Cite web|url=http://club.guns.ru/eng/dragunov.html|archiveurl=https://web.archive.org/web/20090227070644/http://club.guns.ru/eng/dragunov.html|url-status=dead|title=Evgeniy Dragunov: Creator of Firepower (abstracts from a forthcoming book)|archivedate=February 27, 2009}}</ref> The front part of the barrel features the front [[iron sights|sight]] assembly and a [[bayonet]] lug. The muzzle is equipped with a permanently affixed long-slotted [[flash hider]].

In order to pass inspections at the factory, these rifles must not produce more than a 0.7 MOA median deviation from the expected point of impact in three 10-shot groups using 7N1 (approximately 3 MOA).

=== Ammunition ===
To enable the SVD's desired precision, new 7.62×54mmR "sniper" ammunition, designated 7N1, was designed by V. M. Sabelnikov, P. P. Sazonov and V. M. Dvorianinov in 1966 to meet the new standards. 7N1 sniper cartridges should not produce more than 1.24 [[Minute of arc|MOA]] extreme vertical spread with 240&nbsp;mm twist rate barrels and no more than 1.04 MOA extreme vertical spread with 320&nbsp;mm twist rate barrels in a 5-shot group. The precision requirements demanded of the SVD with 7N1 is similar to the American [[M24 Sniper Weapon System]] with M118SB cartridges (1.18 MOA extreme vertical spread) and the [[M110 Semi-Automatic Sniper System]] with M118LR ammunition (1.27 MOA extreme vertical spread).

7N1 differed from the standard LPS Gzh (57-N-323S) cartridge in its use of finely extruded propellant and a modified projectile containing a void inside of the jacket at the tip which improved terminal ballistics and a bimetal lead and mild steel core. With standard 57-N-323S cartridges, the precision of the SVD is reduced to 2.21 MOA extreme vertical spread. This ammunition was later replaced by 7N14 in 1999, which replaced the mild steel penetrator with a hardened steel penetrator in response to the development of infantry body armour.

The SVD was originally issued with a detachable [[PSO-1]] [[telescopic sight|optical sight]] (later upgraded and semi-issued as the PSO-1M2), which mounts to a [[Warsaw Pact rail]] on the left side of the receiver.<ref name="arf.com">{{cite web |url=http://www.ar15.com/content/manuals/pos1.pdf |title=PSO-1 Manual |work=AR15.com |access-date=October 29, 2007 |url-status=dead |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20110518171718/http://www.ar15.com/content/manuals/pos1.pdf |archive-date=May 18, 2011 }}</ref> The PSO-1 elevation turret features [[bullet drop compensation]] (BDC) in {{convert|50|m|yd|0}} or {{convert|100|m|yd|0}} increments for engaging point and area targets at ranges from {{convert|100|m|yd|0}} up to {{convert|1000|m|yd|0}}. The '''Accuracy International AWM''' (Arctic Warfare Magnum or AI-Arctic Warfare Magnum) is a [[bolt-action]] [[sniper rifle]] manufactured by British firearms company [[Accuracy International]] designed for magnum rifle cartridges. The Accuracy International AWM is also unofficially known as the '''AWSM''' (Arctic Warfare Super Magnum), which typically denotes AWM rifles chambered in [[.338 Lapua Magnum]].

On 9 September 2012, Accuracy International announced that the .338 Lapua Magnum AWM rifle was phased out and replaced by the [[Accuracy International Arctic Warfare#AXMC|Accuracy International AXMC sniper rifle]].<ref>{{cite web |url=http://www.accuracyinternational.com/news/?cat= |title=News |date=5 March 2016 |website=Accuracy International |url-status=dead |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20160305011254/http://www.accuracyinternational.com/news/?cat= |archive-date=5 March 2016}}</ref> The bolt action of the AXMC was designed to be significantly stronger and more capable of handling higher chamber pressures and temperatures and thus higher [[bolt thrust]] safely compared to the AWM and hence is longer and wider. The AXMC magazine was also appropriately enlarged to function with .338 Lapua Magnum cartridges loaded to the [[Commission Internationale Permanente pour l'Epreuve des Armes à Feu Portatives]] (C.I.P.) maximum allowed overall length of {{convert|93.50|mm|abbr=on}}.

==Arctic Warfare Magnum System==
{{Main|Accuracy International Arctic Warfare#Design details}}

The Accuracy International AWM sniper rifle is a variant of the British [[Accuracy International Arctic Warfare]] (AW) rifle that was the basis of a [[Accuracy International Arctic Warfare#Rifle system family|family of sniper rifles]] using the Arctic Warfare name. As such the [[Accuracy International Arctic Warfare#Design details|design details]] of the AWM variant are similar to the ones found in the basic AW rifle system. Compared to the AW, the AWM has a longer bolt to accommodate larger magnum-length cartridges such as the [[.300 Winchester Magnum]] and the .338 Lapua Magnum. The bolt head, locking ring, and extractor and magazines were also revised to work with the increased size and operating pressures of magnum rifle cartridges.

The AWM features a detachable single stack [[Magazine (firearm)|removable box magazine]] which holds five rounds. The normal cartridges for this rifle, and the ones which have been accepted by NATO for use in AWM rifles, are .300 Winchester Magnum and .338 Lapua Magnum.

[[Muzzle brake]]s are fitted to reduce recoil, jump and flash and act as a base for optional [[iron sights]] and [[suppressor]]s.

Standard configurations include a [[Schmidt & Bender]] PM II 5-25×56FFP MK II [[telescopic sight]] with P4F graticule but variants with variable magnification of either 3–12×50 or 4–16×50 are also available. Accuracy International actively promotes fitting the German made Schmidt & Bender PM II/MILITARY MK II product line as sighting components on their rifles, which is rare for a rifle manufacturer. The German and Russian Army prefer a telescopic sight made by [[Carl Zeiss AG|Zeiss]].<ref>{{cite web |url=http://www.zeiss.de/C1257088004A21CA/Contents-Frame/A254D374FD464A4CC12570BC002C5992 |title=Telescopic sights for handheld weapons |website=Zeiss |access-date=26 October 2014 |url-status=dead |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20071226144435/http://www.zeiss.de/C1257088004A21CA/Contents-Frame/A254D374FD464A4CC12570BC002C5992 |archive-date=December 26, 2007}}</ref>

The AWM rifle is normally supplied in a metal transit case together with a telescopic sight, mount, butt spacers, bipod, spare magazines, sling, cleaning and tool kits.

==Magnum chamberings==
===.300 Winchester Magnum===
The .300 Winchester Magnum (7.62×67mm) cartridge was designed as a magnum hunting cartridge and offers a flatter trajectory and a significant increase in muzzle velocity, wind resistance and supersonic range over the smaller [[7.62×51mm NATO]] cartridge. The ability of the .300 Winchester Magnum chambering to obtain fairly high muzzle velocities, combined with relatively heavy and long [[very-low-drag bullet]]s, significantly enhance the hit probability at longer ranges and hence the effective range compared to the 7.62×51mm NATO cartridge. The AWM chambered for the .300 is fitted with a fluted, stainless steel barrel that is {{convert|660|mm|abbr=on}} long and features a non-traditional 279.4&nbsp;mm (1:11 in) right-hand twist rate.

===.338 Lapua Magnum===
[[Image:AI AWSM .338 Lap. Mag. Dutch ISAF sniper team.jpg|thumb|right|A Dutch [[International Security Assistance Force|ISAF]] sniper team with an Accuracy International AWM .338 Lapua Magnum rifle.]]
[[File:British and French Snipers During Ex Boars Head.jpg|thumb|A British sniper (centre) carrying his L115A3 Long Range Rifle with attached [[suppressor]], on a joint training mission with French snipers.]]

The AWM in the .338 Lapua Magnum (8.6×70mm) calibre was designed as a dedicated long range sniper rifle. The rifle is fitted with a stainless steel, fluted, {{convert|686|mm|abbr=on}} barrel, which research has found to be the best compromise between muzzle velocity, weight, and length. The barrel has an unconventional 279&nbsp;mm (1:11 in) right-hand twist rate, optimized for firing .338-calibre very-low-drag bullets up to 16.85 g (260 gr). When the AWM .338 Lapua Magnum was developed military issue cartridges were loaded with 16.2 g (250 gr) very-low-drag bullets. Longer, heavier very-low-drag bullets like the Sierra HPBT MatchKing .338-calibre 19.44 g (300 gr) and the 21st century 19.44&nbsp;gram (300 grain) .338-calibre HPBT Scenar can be used, but require a 254&nbsp;mm (1:10 in) twist rate to stabilize them under high air density conditions as found on arctic coasts.<ref>{{cite web |url=http://www.lapua.com/fileadmin/user_upload/esitteet/338LapuaMagnum300grScenar2009.pdf |title=Lapua 19.44 g (300 gr) HPBT Scenar Bullet brochure |website=[[Nammo]] Lapua Oy |url-status=dead |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20101205013955/http://www.lapua.com/fileadmin/user_upload/esitteet/338LapuaMagnum300grScenar2009.pdf |archive-date=2010-12-05}}</ref>

A limitation of AWM rifles is that .338 Lapua Magnum cartridges loaded to the [[Commission Internationale Permanente pour l'Epreuve des Armes à Feu Portatives]] (C.I.P.) maximum allowed overall length of {{convert|93.50|mm|abbr=on}} do not fit in the magazine due to a lack of internal magazine length. This is because the AWM bolt action was initially developed for smaller cartridges, and then modified for the .338 Lapua Magnum chambering.<ref>{{cite web |url=http://www.accuracyinternational.com/news/?p=20 |title=News |website=Accuracy International |access-date=2012-12-25 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20121016075612/http://www.accuracyinternational.com/news/?p=20 |archive-date=2012-10-16 |url-status=dead}}</ref> Ammunition manufacturers produce .338 Lapua Magnum military issue cartridges loaded with 16.2 g (250 gr) very-low-drag bullets (overall length ≤ {{convert|91.44|mm|abbr=on}}) that fit in the {{convert|91.5|mm|abbr=on}} long AWM magazines. As long as .338 Lapua Magnum cartridges that fit in the magazines are used, the AWM rifles can be used as repeating rifles instead of single shot rifles.

To address .338 Lapua Magnum ammunition length limitations of the AWM, Accuracy International has since developed the [[Accuracy International Arctic Warfare#AX series|AX338 long range rifle]] as the AWM successor model. The bolt action of the AX338 is longer and wider than the AWM, and the internal magazine is lengthened, allowing the unimpaired use of .338 Lapua Magnum cartridges loaded to the C.I.P. (Permanent International Commission for the Proof of Firearms Portable) maximum allowed overall length of {{convert|93.5|mm|abbr=on}}. Furthermore, the AX338 has a 238&nbsp;mm (1:9.375&nbsp;in) twist rate to adequately stabilize longer, heavier .338 caliber very-low-drag projectile designs that became more common in the 21st century.<ref>{{cite web |url=http://www.accuracyinternational.com/ax_series.php |title=AX338 .338 long range rifle |website=Accuracy International |access-date=2012-12-23 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20120311122120/http://www.accuracyinternational.com/ax_series.php |archive-date=2012-03-11 |url-status=dead}}</ref><ref>{{cite web |url=http://www.accuracyinternational.com/news/wp-content/uploads/2012/05/How-it-Works-edition-33.pdf |title=How it works magazine article AX338 PSR sniper rifle |website=Accuracy International |access-date=2012-12-25 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20141026103824/http://www.accuracyinternational.com/news/wp-content/uploads/2012/05/How-it-Works-edition-33.pdf |archive-date=2014-10-26 |url-status=dead}}</ref>

Ammunition types available for the .338 Lapua Magnum are [[Full metal jacket bullet|FMJ]], [[hollow point]], [[Armour-piercing shot and shell|armour piercing]] (AP) and armour piercing incendiary (API).{{Citation needed|date=December 2019}}

* [[Suppressor]]s to reduce the flash and noise signature
* Folding stocks for better carrying in a backpack

The MOD claims a muzzle velocity of 936&nbsp;m/s (3,071&nbsp;ft/s) for the L115A3 (another folding stock variant next to the AWM-F).<ref name="L96+L115Rifle">{{cite web |url=http://www.mod.uk/DefenceInternet/FactSheets/L96SniperRifleAndL115a3LongRangeRifle.htm |title=L96 Sniper Rifle and L115A3 Long Range Rifle |website=Ministry of Defence |url-status=dead |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20110607151207/http://www.mod.uk/DefenceInternet/FactSheets/L96SniperRifleAndL115a3LongRangeRifle.htm |archive-date=2011-06-07}}</ref>

When the L115A3 Long Range Rifle was only six years into its life cycle the British Ministry of Defence (MOD) recognized the improved capabilities of the AX series rifles for special forces use. The AX series new chassis system provided more flexibility to utilize (future) low light and daylight aiming optics, laser designators, and other accessories without the need for custom-made mounting interface kits. The right folding AX series chassis system was also more compact in the folded configuration making it more portable and concealable, and featured a pistol grip. On the request of the MOD, Accuracy International explored the possibility of updating the L115A3 Long Range Rifle to the AX series chassis system, to produce a rifle that is a hybrid of AWM and AX features. The AX enhancements were well received by the MOD and British snipers, and an update programme was launched, producing the '''L115A4'''. The L115A4 can easily be mistaken for a new AX series rifle. The Schmidt & Bender 5-25×56&nbsp;mm PM II LP/MILITARY MKII 5-25×56, as well as the suppressor, bipod and several other accessories from the L115A3, were reused in the L115A4, resulting in significant cost savings. Along with the rifle updates, the L115A4 includes a new deployment case, maintenance kit, and other accessories.

The '''SKS''' ({{langx|ru|Самозарядный карабин Симонова, СКС|Samozaryadny karabin Simonova|Simonov self-loading [[carbine]]}}) is a semi-automatic [[carbine]] designed by Soviet small arms designer [[Sergei Gavrilovich Simonov]] in the 1940s.

The SKS was first produced in the [[Soviet Union]] but was later widely exported and manufactured by various nations. Its distinguishing characteristics include a permanently attached folding [[bayonet]] and a hinged, fixed magazine. As the SKS lacked select-fire capability and its magazine was limited to ten rounds, it was rendered obsolete in the [[Eastern Bloc]] by the introduction of the [[AK-47]] series of rifles in the 1950s. Nevertheless, SKS carbines continued to see service with the [[Soviet Border Troops]] and second-line and reserve army units for decades.<ref name="Beckhusen">{{cite news |title=Where Russia's SKS Rifle Bested America's Famous M1 Garand |last=Beckhusen |first=Robert |url=https://nationalinterest.org/blog/reboot/where-russia’s-sks-rifle-bested-america’s-famous-m1-garand-180017 |work=The National Interest |location=Washington DC |date=14 March 2021 |access-date=14 August 2023 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20240223033742/https://nationalinterest.org/blog/reboot/where-russia’s-sks-rifle-bested-america’s-famous-m1-garand-180017 |archive-date=23 February 2024}}</ref>

The SKS was manufactured at [[Tula Arsenal]] from 1949 to 1958, and at the [[Izhevsk Arsenal]] from 1953 to 1954. Altogether, the Soviet Union produced 2.7&nbsp;million SKS carbines.<ref name="Chicom1">{{cite news |title=Is the Chicom Type 56 SKS Rifle Worth the Investment? |last=Dabbs |first=Mike |url=https://www.firearmsnews.com/editorial/type56-sks-rifle/472826 |work=[[Firearms News]] |location=Peoria, Illinois |date=2 May 2023 |access-date=15 May 2024 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20231128165654/https://www.firearmsnews.com/editorial/type56-sks-rifle/472826 |archive-date=28 November 2023}}</ref> Throughout the [[Cold War]], millions of additional SKS carbines and their derivatives were also manufactured under license in the [[People's Republic of China]], as well as a number of countries allied with the Eastern Bloc.<ref name="Chicom1"/> The SKS was exported in vast quantities and found favour with insurgent forces around the world as a utilitarian weapon which was adequate for guerrilla warfare despite its limitations.<ref name="Gander">{{cite book|last=Gander|first=Terry|title=Guerrilla Warfare Weapons: The Modern Underground Fighter's Armoury|year=1990|pages=52, 80|publisher=Sterling Publishing Company|location=New York City|isbn=978-0806973333}}</ref>

Beginning in 1988, millions have also been sold on the civilian market in [[Canada]] and the [[United States]], where they remain popular as hunting and sporting rifles.

== Design ==
[[File:SKS magazine.JPG|thumb|left|SKS with the magazine closed (top) and open. The magazine release is circled. The release gets pulled back to open the built-in magazine.]]
[[File:SKS bayonet noBG.jpg|thumb|left|An SKS with a blade-type [[bayonet]] in its closed (folded back) and open positions.]]
[[File:SKS field strip.jpg|thumb|left|A [[Field strip|field-stripped]] SKS carbine (disassembled into major components for cleaning).]]

The SKS is a [[Gas-operated reloading|gas-operated]] carbine with a conventional wooden stock and a fixed ten-round box magazine enclosed inside the receiver.<ref name=Jane98/> It has a tilting bolt and a [[Gas-operated reloading#Short-stroke fixed|gas piston operating rod]] that works to unlock and cycle the action via gas pressure.<ref name=Jane98/> When a round is discharged, some of the gases in the bore are diverted through the gas port and impinge on the head of the piston.<ref name=Jane98/> The piston is driven rearwards and the tappet strikes the bolt carrier; a spring returns the tappet and piston to their forward position.<ref name=Jane98/> The bolt carrier is driven rearwards, which causes it to lift and unlock the bolt and allowing it to be carried rearwards against the recoil spring.<ref name=Jane98/> This allows the fired cartridge case to be ejected, and as the bolt is returned to its original position by the recoil spring it strips a new round from the magazine and chambers it.<ref name=Jane98/>  

The SKS magazine can be loaded either by hand or from a [[stripper clip]] which seats in the bolt carrier.<ref name=Jane98/> To load the rifle, the cocking handle on the right of the bolt is retracted, and if the magazine is empty the bolt will remain at the rear.<ref name=Jane98/> When the magazine is fully loaded, the bolt is pulled slightly back then released, at which time it will chamber the first round.<ref name=Jane98/> Cartridges stored in the magazine can be removed by pulling back on a latch located forward of the trigger guard (thus opening the "floor" of the magazine and allowing the rounds to fall out).<ref name="pdf.textfiles">{{cite report |title=SKS RIFLE, Simonov Type 56 |series=TC 9–56 |publisher=Headquarters, Department of the Army |date=1 October 1969 |url=http://pdf.textfiles.com/manuals/MILITARY/united_states_army_tc_9-56%20-%201_october_1969.pdf |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20121224023008/http://pdf.textfiles.com/manuals/MILITARY/united_states_army_tc_9-56%20-%201_october_1969.pdf |archive-date=24 December 2012}}</ref>

When the magazine is expended, a small stud extension on the magazine follower engages the bolt hold open, locking the bolt to the rear.<ref name=Jane98/> After the magazine follower is depressed by the insertion of ammunition or opened from the bottom, the stud continues to hold the bolt at the rear of the receiver until the bolt is pulled back, at which time it drops into its normal position and releases the bolt to chamber the next round.<ref name=Jane98/>

While early (1949–50) Soviet models had spring-loaded firing pins, which held the pin away from cartridge primers until struck by the action's hammer, most variants of the SKS have a free-floating firing pin within the bolt. Because of this design, care must be taken during cleaning (especially after long storage packed in [[cosmoline]]) to ensure that the firing pin can freely move and does not stick in the forward position within the bolt. SKS firing pins that are stuck in the forward position have been known to cause accidental "[[Slamfire|slamfires]]" (the rifle firing on its own, without pulling the trigger and often without being fully locked). This behavior is less likely with the hard primer military-spec ammo for which the SKS was designed, but as with any rifle, users should properly maintain their firearms. For collectors, slamfires are more likely when the bolt still has remnants of cosmoline embedded in it that retard firing pin movement. As it is triangular in cross section with only one way to properly insert it (notches up), slamfires can also result if the firing pin is inserted in one of the other two orientations.

SKS rifles, with the exception of the Yugoslavian variant, the barrel is chrome-lined for increased wear and heat tolerance from sustained fire and to resist corrosion from chlorate-primed corrosive ammunition, as well as to facilitate easier bore cleaning.

The front sight has a hooded post. The rear sight is an open notch type which is adjustable for elevation from {{convert|100|to|1000|m|yd}}. There is also an all-purpose "battle" setting on the sight ladder (marked "П", for "Прямой выстрел", meaning "Straight shot"), set for {{convert|300|m|yd|abbr=off}}. This is attained by moving the elevation slide to the rear of the ladder as far as it will go.<ref name="pdf.textfiles" /><ref>{{Cite web |url=http://www.pistolcraft.com/sks/ |title=SKS Instruction Manual<!-- Bot generated title --> |access-date=2012-04-25 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20120411103553/http://www.pistolcraft.com/sks/ |archive-date=2012-04-11 |url-status=live }}</ref> 

All military SKSs have a [[bayonet]] attached to the underside of the barrel, which is extended and retracted via a spring-loaded hinge. Both blade and spike bayonets were produced.<ref name="pdf.textfiles" /> Spike bayonets were used on the 1949 Tula Russian SKS-45, the Chinese Type 56 from mid 1964 onward, and the Albanian Model 561.<ref name="pdf.textfiles" /> 

The SKS is easily field stripped and reassembled without specialized tools,<!-- unfired cartidges are not needed for field stripping, the trigger group does not need to be removed to field strip the gun--> and the trigger group and magazine can be removed with an unfired cartridge, or with the receiver cover. The rifle has a cleaning kit stored in a trapdoor in the buttstock, with a cleaning rod running under the barrel, in the same style as the AK-47. The cap for the cleaning kit also serves as a cleaning rod guide, to protect the crown from being damaged during cleaning. The body of the cleaning kit serves as the cleaning rod handle. In common with some other Soviet-era designs, it trades some accuracy for ruggedness, reliability, ease of maintenance, ease of use, and low manufacturing cost.

==Development history==

The Soviet Union utilized a number of semi-automatic as well as select-fire rifles during [[World War II]], namely the [[AVS-36]], [[SVT-38]], and [[SVT-40]].<ref name="Smith1">{{cite journal |last=Smith |first=Joseph |title=Current Soviet small arms |journal=[[Infantry (magazine)|Infantry]] |pages=21–23 |volume=51| issue=2|publisher=[[United States Army Infantry School]] |location=Fort Moore, Georgia |date=March–April 1961}}</ref> However, the primary service rifle of the [[Red Army]] remained the bolt-action [[Mosin–Nagant]], which fired the powerful but heavy [[7.62×54mmR]] round.<ref name="Smith1"/> Even prior to the war, the Red Army had recognized that these weapons were obsolete and initiated a program to modernize its existing small arms, although this was interrupted by the [[Operation Barbarossa|German invasion of the Soviet Union in 1941]].<ref name="Johnson1">{{cite journal |last=Johnson |first=Harold |title=Current Soviet small arms |journal=Army Research and Development |pages=31–32 |volume=15 |issue=6|publisher=[[United States Army Materiel Command]] |location=Alexandria, Virginia |date=March–April 1961}}</ref> Among the military development programs the Soviet Union had monitored in other countries were the Finnish, Swiss, and German developments in intermediate rifle cartridges.<ref name="Johnson1"/> These had limited range and muzzle velocity compared to the 7.62×54mmR and other contemporary rifle rounds such as the [[7.92×57mm Mauser]] and the [[.30-06 Springfield]], but also possessed numerous advantages: they were cheaper to manufacture, permitted easier weapons handling due to their much-reduced recoil and muzzle blast, and enabled infantry to carry more due to their small size and light weight.<ref name="Smith1"/> They could also be fired from shorter and lighter rifles.<ref name="Smith1"/> The Red Army's interest in an [[intermediate cartridge]] was piqued when stocks of [[7.92×33mm Kurz]] ammunition were captured Hurried efforts were made to introduce a rifle capable of firing the new cartridge, and the first prominent design was offered by [[Sergei Gavrilovich Simonov]].<ref name="Chivers"/> This was known as the ''Samozaryadny Karabin sistemy Simonova'' (SKS), or "Simonov's self-loading carbine system" <ref name="Chivers"/> Simonov had already been working on a semi-automatic carbine chambered for a lighter cartridge as early as 1941, owing to recent complaints about the effectiveness of the SVT-40.<ref name=Ezell/> In fact, one of his earliest prototypes was chambered for the [[7.62×25mm Tokarev]] pistol cartridge, which was also used in the [[PPSh-41]] submachine gun.<ref name=Ezell/> He also built at least one prototype chambered for the larger 7.62×54mmR cartridge.<ref name=Ezell/> Unlike previous Soviet semi-automatic rifles, these utilized fixed five or ten-round magazines loaded from stripper clips.<ref name=Ezell/> They were also distinguished by a large muzzle brake and a fixed gas system covered with a metal shroud.<ref name=Bolotin/> Simonov's design was based on the operating mechanism of the [[PTRS-41]] anti-tank rifle he'd previously developed for the Red Army the same year.<ref name="Smith1"/> On 1 July 1941, the Artillery Committee of the Red Army noted in its records that the Simonov's self-loading carbine, designated SKS-41, satisfied its basic "tactical and technical requirements".<ref name=Bolotin/> The Committee praised the SKS-41 for its light weight and the design of its fixed magazine; it recommended that 50 pre-production models with ten-round magazines be presented to the Red Army for trials.<ref name=Bolotin/> The SKS-41 was to be chambered for the 7.62×54mmR cartridge for logistical reasons, as the Soviet government wished to adapt its existing rifle barrel production lines for the new carbine.<ref name="Walter05">{{cite book|last=Walter|first=John|title=Guns of the Elite Forces|year=2005|pages=41, 118–120|publisher=Greenhill Books|location=London|isbn=978-1853676376}}</ref>
